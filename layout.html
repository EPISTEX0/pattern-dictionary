Hero centered

Centered hero. The first screen stacks every block on one vertical center axis: a small label, a large headline, one line of description, two buttons, and a product shot partly visible at the bottom edge. The eye has a single reading direction.

Use whenthe page has one message and one primary action: a product landing page, a launch page, an early-access signup. The headline is short and strong enough to stand alone.

Avoid whenusers need to compare several equal options on the first screen, or the headline runs longer than 2 lines. Centered multi-line text is hard to read.

Split hero

Split hero. The first screen divides into two balanced halves: copy on the left (message and call to action), image on the right (product or illustration). Users read the message first, then look at the image to confirm it. On mobile, the halves stack: copy first, image second.

Use whenthe product needs to be seen right away (an app, a device, a card, a dashboard) and the message takes a few sentences to explain. This is the most common hero layout in SaaS and fintech.

Avoid whenthere is no strong image. Half a screen of stock photography weakens the whole first screen.

Bento grid

Bento grid. Tiles of different sizes fit tightly into one rectangle. As a layout, the main rule is tile size matches importance: the main feature takes a 2×2 tile, the second feature a wide 2×1 tile, and supporting content 1×1 tiles. Bento as a visual style is covered on the Visual styles page.

Use whenyou present 4–7 features with a clear hierarchy on one screen, so users see the main feature and the supporting ones at a glance.

Avoid whenthe items are equal in importance (use a card grid), or the tiles have no visual content of their own. A large tile with only text looks empty.

Asymmetric grid

Asymmetric grid. Still built on 12 columns, but blocks are neither evenly divided nor centered: the headline overlaps the image, the paragraph starts offset inward, the caption sits in a corner. Empty space is part of the design.

Use whenthe page tells a story: magazines, portfolios, brands that want an editorial layout instead of a template. With the grid on, the blocks are offset but every edge still aligns to a column.

Avoid whenthe page is data-heavy or a form, where users need to scan quickly and predict where information sits.

Full-bleed

Full-bleed. Text sits in a narrow column for readability, alternating with images or color bands that extend to both edges of the screen. The narrow, wide, narrow rhythm makes the space feel open. Scroll inside the frame.

Use whenyou publish long articles, case studies or brand pages with high-quality images, and need large visual breaks between sections of text.

Avoid whenthe images are low resolution or lack a focal point. Full-bleed size makes the flaws obvious.

Card grid

Card grid. Cards share one size and one structure (image, label, title) and wrap to fit the screen width. It is the most template-like layout on the web, which makes it predictable and easy to use. Tap a filter to see the grid rearrange.

Use whenthe items are truly equal and users need to browse, compare and filter: product catalogs, blogs, recipes, search results. Here, the template feel is an advantage.

Avoid whenone item matters far more than the rest (use a bento grid or a hero), or there are only 3 features to present. Three identical cards are a common sign of AI-generated interfaces.

Prompt tip: to avoid the template look, do not just write "card grid". Say which item takes priority, or ask for a featured card that spans 2 columns.
