Onboarding

Patterns that take new users from their first visit to the moment they see the value of the product, without reading documentation. Four levels of intervention, from light to strong: hint dots, hidden advanced options, a task checklist, and a step-by-step tour.

Coachmarks / Hotspots

Hint dots and tip bubbles. Small pulsing dots (hotspots) sit next to features that are new or easy to miss. The user decides when to select one, and a tip bubble (coachmark) appears; once seen, the dot stops pulsing but stays available. Close a tip with “Got it”, Esc, or a click outside. Click or tap each orange dot, or Tab to a dot and press Space.

Avoid whena screen would need many dots (keep it to a few), or the content is a set of required steps in order: use a product tour or an onboarding checklist instead. Dots should stop pulsing once the user has seen them.

Prompt tip: distinguish hotspots (dots the user selects) from tour tooltips (steps that appear in sequence on their own). Ask for “dismiss on Esc and outside click, remember seen state”.

Progressive disclosure

Showing options as they are needed. The interface shows only the options most people need; the rest appear when the user picks a related option or opens an advanced section. New users are not overwhelmed, and experienced users still have every tool. Change “General access”, open “Advanced options”, turn on an expiration date or a password, and watch the control counter.

Onboarding checklist

Getting-started task list. A small panel lists 3–6 tasks that lead a new user to the moment they see the product’s value, with a progress bar. The first task is already checked off to build momentum. Each completed task updates the app and opens the next one. Press the blue button in each task, try “Skip” on the optional task, and minimize the panel with the arrow button.

Use whenpeople must complete a few setup steps before they see value (create a project, invite members, connect data). Starting with progress already made (endowed progress, the Zeigarnik effect) encourages people to finish the remaining steps.

Avoid whenthe product works right away without setup, or the list is longer than 6–7 tasks. Do not enforce a strict order or let the panel take up space indefinitely: allow minimizing, skipping, and hiding it entirely once complete.

Product tour

Step-by-step guided tour. On the first visit to the app, a sequence of short cards highlights each main area in turn (the rest is dimmed) and explains it in one sentence. The tour always has a Skip button, allows going back a step, and shows the current step. Move between steps with the buttons or the ← → keys, and press Esc to exit; the ? button in the top bar reopens the tour. Press “Start” to begin.

Use whenthe interface has a few areas that do not explain themselves and new users need to know where they are before starting. Keep the tour short (3–5 steps), one idea per step, run it once, and make it possible to reopen.

Avoid whenthe tour compensates for a confusing interface, or runs to 10 steps so that people press “Next” just to get through it. Always keep a “Skip” button. Letting people do the tasks themselves (a checklist) usually works better than watching an introduction.

Prompt tip: ask for a “spotlight cutout overlay”, “step counter 2/4”, “Skip / Back / Next”, “arrow keys and Esc”, “focus moves into tour card”, and “relaunch from help menu”.
