Dark patterns

A dark pattern is an interface design that steers users toward a choice that works against their own interest. This page describes seven common types so that you can recognize and avoid them. It is not a guide to using them. Each demo is a fictional example with a Deceptive ↔ Honest switch that shows the same screen designed correctly.

Confirmshaming

Decline copy written to make the user feel guilty. The option to decline is phrased as a self-criticism, such as “No, I prefer to miss out…”. It is usually small, low in contrast, and placed below a prominent primary action. Tap the decline option in both versions.

Fictional example · Deceptive version

How to spot itThe decline copy blames or mocks the user, the decline option is much smaller and paler than the accept button, or the dialog has no close button.

Do this insteadUse two buttons of equal size with neutral labels (“Subscribe” / “No thanks”) and a visible × close button. Shaming copy leaves users with a negative impression of the brand. In the EU, the Digital Services Act (DSA) prohibits platforms from designing interfaces that manipulate user choices.

Hidden costs

Mandatory fees appear only at the final step. The price at the start of checkout is low. Service, processing and shipping fees appear only on the payment screen, after the user has entered their details and is less willing to go back. Tap Continue through all three steps and watch the total.

Fictional example · Deceptive version

How to spot itThe total at payment is higher than the price shown at the start, or unexplained fees (“service fee”, “processing fee”) appear without prior notice.

Do this insteadShow the final total price, including tax, all mandatory fees and estimated shipping, on the product page or in the cart, and keep it unchanged through payment. The EU, the UK and the US (for live-event tickets and short-term lodging) require the total price to be shown upfront.

Roach motel

Easy to sign up, hard to cancel. Signing up takes one tap. Canceling requires finding a hidden link, answering several screens of questions, declining a retention offer, and finally calling a support line. Upgrade to Pro, then try to cancel. The counter above the card records each tap.

Fictional example · Deceptive version

How to spot itSign-up buttons appear in many places but the cancel option is hard to find, canceling takes far more steps than signing up, a survey is mandatory, or cancellation is possible only by phone, by email, or during business hours.

Do this insteadPlace “Cancel plan” next to the plan details, followed by one confirmation screen that states what happens and from which date. A retention offer, if any, is a secondary option. Germany, California and other jurisdictions require online cancellation to be as easy as sign-up.

Forced continuity

A free trial converts to a paid plan without notice. The trial requires a card, the renewal price sits in very small print, and no reminder email is sent before the charge. Tap Start, then tap Next day until day 8.

Fictional example · Deceptive version

How to spot it“Free” is prominent, but the post-trial price and the billing start date are small or missing, no reminder is sent before the charge, and the way to cancel is hidden.

Do this insteadState “$0 today, $Y/mo from date X”, send a reminder email before billing starts, and let users cancel the trial in one tap. Many automatic renewal laws, such as California’s, require the price, renewal date and cancellation method to be disclosed clearly.

Fake urgency / scarcity

Countdown timers and stock counts that do not reflect reality. Time pressure pushes users to buy before they compare options. In the deceptive version, tap Reload page: the timer resets to 10:00 and the count stays at 2.

Fictional example · Deceptive version

How to spot itThe timer restarts every time the page opens, “only N left” never decreases, “X people are viewing” changes at random, or an offer that “ends today” appears every day.

Do this insteadGive the deadline as a fixed date and time, and take seat or stock counts from real system data. If no data exists, show nothing. In the EU, falsely stating that an offer is available only for a very short time is a prohibited unfair commercial practice.

Sneak into basket / Preselected opt-in

Add-ons are placed in the cart automatically and consent boxes are pre-checked. Insurance, warranties or optional services are pre-checked or added directly to the order, and the marketing consent box is pre-checked too. Users who do not read carefully pay for items they did not choose. Check or uncheck the options and watch the total.

Fictional example · Deceptive version

How to spot itThe cart contains items the user did not add, boxes for paid extras or marketing consent are already checked, and the “remove” link is small and low in contrast.

Do this insteadLeave every paid option and every consent box unchecked by default, and show the price next to each option. In the EU, pre-checked boxes for extra payments are prohibited, and a pre-checked box is not valid consent under the GDPR.

Nagging

The same request repeats until the user agrees. Prompts to enable notifications, rate the app or upgrade appear again and again, interrupt the current task, and offer only “Maybe later” instead of “Don’t ask again”. Check a few tasks in the list.

Fictional example · Deceptive version

How to spot itA dialog with the same content appears several times in one session, blocks the current task, and the only alternative to “Accept” is “Maybe later”.

Do this insteadAsk once, at a moment with a clear reason (for example, when the user sets a due date). Do not block the screen, offer “No thanks”, and respect that answer. Provide a switch in Settings so users can change their mind. Users who are asked repeatedly often decline permanently or uninstall the app.
